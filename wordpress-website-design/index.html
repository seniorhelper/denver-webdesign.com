The actual question

Do you need WordPress, or do you need to publish?

Almost everyone who asks for WordPress is really asking for one thing: the ability to change the site themselves. That's a completely reasonable want. It's just worth knowing it's the only real reason, because it comes with a maintenance bill attached.

Decision
Choose WordPress

You genuinely publish, regularly

Not "we might blog someday." Actually posting — articles, listings, events, case studies — often enough that waiting on someone else would stop you doing it.

  • Multiple people update the site
  • You post weekly or monthly, consistently
  • You need a specific plugin nothing else replicates
  • A membership or forum sits on the site
Choose static instead

You want the site to just work

Pages that describe your services and rarely change. If you'd rather send us an edit and have it done that afternoon than log into an admin panel, static is faster, cheaper to run and has nothing to hack.

  • Content changes a few times a year
  • Speed on a phone is the priority
  • Nobody on your team wants to be a webmaster
  • You'd rather not think about updates at all

Why this matters
01

Bots find you, not people

Small business sites are rarely targeted deliberately. They get swept up by automated scans hunting one known vulnerability in one outdated plugin across the entire internet.

02

The damage is quiet

A compromised site often keeps working while serving spam links to Google. Owners usually find out from a search warning weeks later, after the rankings have gone.

03

Updates break things too

The reason people stop updating is that an update once broke the site. Which is real — it's why maintenance means testing, not just clicking the button.

If WordPress is right

How we build it so it doesn't rot

Most WordPress sites are slow and fragile because of decisions made in the first week. These are the ones that matter.

Build principles
01

Fewest plugins that do the job

Every plugin is code someone else maintains, a possible conflict and a possible vulnerability. If two lines of custom code replace a plugin, we write the two lines.

02

No page builder if we can avoid it

Drag-and-drop builders generate enormous amounts of markup and lock your content into their format. Leaving one later usually means rebuilding the site.

03

A lean theme, not a demo

Multipurpose themes ship dozens of layouts you'll never use and load the weight anyway. A focused theme beats a feature-packed one every time.

04

Caching and image handling from day one

Not bolted on when someone complains about speed. Modern formats, correct sizing, and pages served from cache rather than rebuilt for every visitor.

05

Login hardened and moved

Strong credentials, two-factor, failed-attempt limits, and the login not sitting at the default address every bot on earth already knows.

06

Backups you have actually tested

An untested backup is a hope, not a plan. Off-site, automatic, and restored once deliberately so you know it works before you need it.

Then, every month

Core, theme and plugin updates applied and checked rather than trusted. Broken links and errors reviewed. Backups verified. On our monthly plans this is included, because the alternative is you not doing it — which is the state most WordPress sites end up in. See pricing.

Straight answers

WordPress questions

How much does a WordPress website cost?

The build sits in normal website territory — published 2026 research puts most professional small business sites at $3,000 to $15,000, and our monthly plans start at $99. The part people miss is maintenance, which is genuinely required on WordPress and costs either money or your own time every single month.

Is WordPress good for SEO?

It can be, and it isn't automatic. WordPress gives you the controls; whether the site is fast, well structured and properly marked up depends entirely on the theme, the plugins and the person who built it. A bloated WordPress site is harder to rank than a well-built static one, and vice versa.

How many plugins is too many?

There's no magic number, but every plugin adds code, a possible conflict and a possible vulnerability. Most sites we look at are running at least three doing something the theme already does or that nobody uses. The right test is whether each one earns its risk.

Do I really need to update WordPress?

Yes. The overwhelming majority of compromised small business sites are taken through a known vulnerability in an outdated plugin, found by an automated scan rather than a person. Updates are how those get closed. Skipping them is the single most common reason a site gets hacked.

My WordPress site is slow. Can it be fixed?

Usually. Start by identifying whether it's plugins, images, the theme or the hosting — they need different fixes and guessing wastes money. If the site is built on a heavy page builder with twenty-five plugins, honest answer is that rebuilding often costs less than repeatedly patching.

Should I use a page builder like Elementor or Divi?

They make editing easier, which is a real benefit. They also add significant weight to every page and lock your content into their format, so leaving later usually means rebuilding. If editing yourself is the whole reason you chose WordPress, that trade may be worth it — just make it knowingly.

Can you take over my existing WordPress site?

Yes. We'd want to look at what's running first, because a healthy site needing tidy-up and a site needing replacement are very different jobs at very different prices. Bring us admin and hosting access and you'll get a straight assessment.

What happens if my site gets hacked?

Clean-up means removing the injected code, closing the hole that let it in, and dealing with any search penalty that followed. It is always more expensive and more stressful than the maintenance that would have prevented it. Tested backups make the difference between a bad afternoon and a bad month.

Can I move from WordPress to something faster?

Often yes, and it makes sense when you've realised you never actually publish. The critical part is keeping the URLs Google has indexed and redirecting properly. More on migrating without losing rankings.

Do you build WooCommerce stores?

Yes, and it fits best when the store sits inside a content site you already publish to. If selling is the main event, Shopify is usually less work to run. Both compared here.
