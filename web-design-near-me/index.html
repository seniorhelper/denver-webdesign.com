The part nobody explains

What "near me" actually means to Google

You don't have to type a city. Google already knows roughly where you are — from your phone's location, your network, or your past searches. Then it decides which businesses qualify as near you using three things, weighted together.

Proximity

How physically close the business is to wherever you are right now. This is why the same search gives different results in Arvada and in Parker, and why a company two suburbs over can outrank a better one further away.

Relevance

How well the business matches what you asked for. A company whose site clearly says it does web design for small businesses in Denver is a better match than one whose homepage says "digital solutions for the modern enterprise."

Prominence

How well known and well regarded the business appears to be — reviews, mentions, links, how established the site is. This is the slowest of the three to build and the hardest to fake.

Worth knowing

Proximity is why "near me" is winnable

A national search term like "website designer" puts you against Wix, Squarespace and Fiverr. Add "near me" and Google throws almost all of that out, because those aren't near anybody. You're left competing with the local businesses actually around you — a completely different and much shorter fight.

For your business

How to show up when your customers search "near me"

This is the same problem from the other side, and it's most of what a local business actually needs from a website. None of it is secret and you can start on several of these today without hiring anybody.

Do first

Claim your Google Business Profile

For a local service business this is often more important than the website itself, because the map results sit above the organic ones. Free, and most of the work is filling it in properly and keeping the hours right.

Do first

Get your NAP identical everywhere

Name, address and phone, written the same way on your site, your profile and every directory you're listed in. Inconsistency here quietly undermines everything else you do.

Do first

Say where you work, in words

Not a list of towns in the footer. Actual sentences in your content naming the areas you serve, on pages that mean something. Google reads the page, not the keyword stuffing.

Then

Make the phone number tappable

Visible before anyone scrolls, on every page, as a link that dials. A "near me" searcher is usually on a phone and usually in a hurry. Making them hunt for the number loses them.

Then

Add LocalBusiness schema

Structured data stating your service area, hours and phone in a form machines read directly. It's how search engines and AI assistants stop guessing where you are.

Then

Build real service-area pages

One page per area you genuinely work in, each with a reason to exist beyond the town name. Twenty thin pages with a swapped city is a pattern Google has recognised for years.

"Near me" questions

How does Google decide what counts as near me?

It combines three things: how physically close a business is to you, how well it matches what you searched for, and how prominent it appears to be based on reviews, mentions and links. Proximity is why the same search gives different results in different suburbs, and why a business a few miles away can outrank a better one further out.

Do I need to type my city in the search?

No. Google already has a good idea where you are from your device and network. Adding the city sometimes changes results slightly, because it signals you want that area specifically rather than wherever you happen to be standing. Both are worth checking if you're researching who to hire.

Is a local web designer better than a remote one?

Only when local knowledge affects the work. If your customers search locally, someone who knows the market genuinely helps. If you sell nationally, geography buys you very little and you should pick on the work instead. Anyone who says local is always better is selling you their address.

How do I get my own business to show up in "near me" searches?

Start with a properly completed Google Business Profile, because the map results sit above the organic ones for most local searches. Then make sure your name, address and phone are identical everywhere, your site says in plain sentences where you work, and your phone number is tappable on every page. Those four things cover most of it.

Do I need a separate page for every suburb?

Only for areas you genuinely work in, and only if each page has a real reason to exist. Twenty near-identical pages with the town name swapped is a pattern Google has recognised and discounted for years. Three or four substantial area pages beat twenty thin ones every time.
