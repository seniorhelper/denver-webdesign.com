Inside the invoice

Where the money actually goes

On a typical custom project, design accounts for roughly 30–40% of the cost and development 50–60%, with testing and launch making up the rest. So a $10,000 website is around $3,500 of design and $5,500 of build.

Design 35%
Development 55%
Launch 10%

Bundling design and development together is usually cheaper than hiring two suppliers, because nothing is lost in the handoff and nobody bills twice for the same conversation.

What pushes the number up

Biggest factor

Design originality

A template-led site reuses proven layouts. A genuinely custom design involves 20 to 40 hours of research and visual work. That gap explains most of the difference between a $3,500 site and a $14,000 one.

Biggest factor

Page count

Every page needs a layout, content and testing. A five-page site and a thirty-page site are not remotely the same job, even when the design is identical.

Biggest factor

Functionality

A contact form is trivial. Booking with real availability, payments, logins or software integrations are software projects sitting inside a website project.

Add-on

Content and copywriting

Professional copywriting typically runs $50 to $150 an hour. Many quotes assume you'll supply the words, which is also the thing most likely to stall the project.

Add-on

Photography

A shoot runs roughly $500 to $2,500. Often worth it, because original images build more trust than stock with customers and with search engines alike.

Add-on

Search optimization

Structure should be part of the build. Ongoing campaigns are a separate retainer. Ask any supplier to split those two clearly, because blurring them is common.

The part that isn't in the quote

Running costs nobody mentions

Published research puts the ongoing costs most owners overlook at $1,100 to $5,000 a year — hosting, security, backups, maintenance and the tools that keep the thing working. One analysis of a representative $28,000 marketing site with a mid-tier maintenance program put the three-year total between $65,000 and $95,000.

Practical

How not to overpay

Most overspending isn't caused by a dishonest supplier. It's caused by buyers comparing quotes that describe completely different projects.

Write the scope before you ask for quotes

A $5,000 template refresh and a $150,000 rebuild with a dozen integrations both get called "a website redesign." If you request three quotes without defining the project first, you'll be comparing numbers that don't describe the same work. Write down your page count, your must-have features and your deadline, then send the same brief to everyone.

Ask for the three-year figure, not the build price

Build plus hosting plus maintenance plus edits plus every recurring fee. A supplier who can't produce that number hasn't thought about your budget, only about winning the job.

Don't buy functionality you can't name a use for

Booking systems, portals and integrations are where budgets disappear. If you can't describe the specific weekly task it removes, you don't need it yet. Building the simple version and adding later is almost always cheaper than the reverse.

Separate structure from campaigns

Technical search structure belongs in the build. Ongoing content and link campaigns are a separate service. Suppliers who quote "SEO included" without splitting those are usually including the first and letting you assume the second.

Check what a small change costs

You'll need dozens of small edits over the years. If each one is a ticket, an estimate and a two-week wait, the real cost of the site is much higher than the quote suggests — or you simply stop updating it, which is worse.

Run PageSpeed on their own website

Free, thirty seconds, and they can't argue with it. A supplier whose own site is slow on mobile is showing you their standard before you've paid anything.

Straight answers

Website cost questions

How much does a website cost in 2026?

Most professional small business websites land between $3,000 and $15,000, with freelancers typically $3,000 to $8,000 and boutique agencies $8,000 to $15,000. DIY builders run $10 to $50 a month. Custom ecommerce at scale runs $45,000 to $250,000, and web applications start around $50,000. Our custom-coded sites start at $99 a month.

Why is there such a huge range?

Because "a website" describes wildly different products. A five-page brochure site and a platform with payments, logins and integrations are different disciplines. Within the small business band, the gap comes down to design originality, content work and how much technical structure is included.

What is the cheapest way to get a website?

A DIY builder, roughly $10 to $50 a month, or around $5.99 on the plans we resell. You do the work and it will look like a template. That's genuinely fine if the site is a placeholder. It's a poor choice if the website is supposed to bring in customers, because you can't control the code underneath.

Is a more expensive website better?

Not reliably. Price correlates with design originality and scope, not with whether the thing gets you customers. Plenty of expensive sites are slow, poorly structured and invisible in search. The test is whether it loads fast, gets found and makes contacting you obvious — and you can check all three for free.

How much should ongoing costs be?

Research puts the commonly overlooked running costs at $1,100 to $5,000 a year across hosting, security, backups, maintenance and tools. Hosting alone can be as little as $2 a month for a small site. If a supplier's ongoing fees sit far above that band, ask specifically what the money buys.

Should I pay monthly or one lump sum?

Both are legitimate. A lump sum means you own the site outright and can walk away. Monthly removes the upfront barrier and usually bundles hosting and maintenance, but you need to check what happens at the end and whether you eventually own it. Ask that question before signing either way.

Does the price include content and photos?

Frequently not, and it's the most common gap in a quote. Copywriting runs $50 to $150 an hour and a photo shoot $500 to $2,500. Ask explicitly who is writing the words, because "client to supply content" is what stalls most website projects.

How much does a website cost per month to run?

On a builder, $10 to $50. For a custom site, hosting from a few dollars plus whatever maintenance arrangement you have. Published guidance puts ongoing costs for custom sites at roughly $50 to $200 a month once maintenance is included. Ours is $99 with everything bundled.

What does a website cost in Denver specifically?

Denver agencies commonly quote $9,000 to $22,000 for a professional five-page site, which sits at the upper end of national ranges. Our Denver pricing is published and starts at $99 a month.

Can I build a website myself and save the money?

Yes, and for some businesses it's the right call. Budget a weekend or two, accept that it will look like a template, and be aware that search performance is limited by the platform. If the site is a formality, that's a fine trade. If it's meant to generate work, you're trading money for a much slower result.
