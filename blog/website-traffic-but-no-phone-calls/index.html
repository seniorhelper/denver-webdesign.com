Conversion

Your website gets traffic. So why isn't the phone ringing?

This is the most common thing a business owner tells us, and it's almost always misdiagnosed. People arrive, and then nothing happens. The instinct is to get more visitors. Usually the visitors were never the problem.

7 min read · Denver Web Design

If a hundred people land on your site this month and nobody calls, doubling that to two hundred gets you twice as many people not calling. The leak is between arriving and acting, and until that's fixed, every dollar spent on traffic leaks out the same hole.

Here are the six causes we find most often, roughly in the order they're worth checking. The first three are free to diagnose and take about twenty minutes total.

1. The phone number isn't visible without scrolling

Open your site on your own phone. Don't scroll. Can you see a number, and does tapping it start a call?

An astonishing number of small business sites fail this. The number sits in the footer, or it's part of a header image so it can't be tapped, or it's there but grey on grey. Somebody who arrived ready to call now has to hunt, and a meaningful share of them won't.

Your number should be visible before any scrolling, on every page, as a real link that dials. Not a graphic. Not just on the contact page.

2. The page doesn't say what you actually do

Read your homepage headline out loud. If it says something like "delivering quality solutions with a commitment to excellence," a visitor cannot tell what you sell, where you work, or whether you're relevant to them.

They have one question when they land: is this the right business for my problem? If the first line doesn't answer it, they hit back and try the next result. That decision takes a couple of seconds.

Bathroom remodeling in Denver. Licensed and insured. Most projects finished in under two weeks.

That headline is worse writing and dramatically better business. It answers the question immediately.

3. There's no price and no range anywhere

The single most common reason someone leaves a small business website is not knowing whether they can afford you. They won't call to find out. Calling means a conversation, and possibly a sales pitch, and possibly embarrassment.

You don't need a full price list. A starting figure, a typical range, or one worked example removes the largest source of hesitation on the page. Businesses consistently worry this will scare people off. What it actually does is stop the wrong people calling and give the right ones permission to.

4. Every service is crammed onto one page

If you do three things and they share a page, that page is competing for three different searches and winning none of them cleanly. Worse, a visitor searching for one specific service arrives and has to scan past two others to find themselves.

One page per service. Each one written for the person searching that specific thing. This is usually the highest-return change available to a small business site, and it helps rankings and conversion simultaneously.

5. The site is slow on a phone

Run your homepage through Google's free PageSpeed Insights tool and read the mobile tab. If Performance comes back under 50, you're losing people before they see anything at all.

Most visitors are on a phone, often on patchy signal. A heavy theme with a video background and a stack of plugins can take five or six seconds to become usable, and a large share of people won't wait. They don't bounce angrily; they just never arrive.

6. There's no obvious next step

Look at any page on your site and ask what you want the visitor to do. If the answer is "read it," that's the problem.

Every page needs one clear action, and it should almost always be the same one. Call. Not "call, or fill in this form, or email, or download our brochure, or follow us." Choice creates hesitation. One obvious action, repeated, converts better than five competing ones.

The quickest test

Hand your phone to someone who doesn't know your business, open your homepage, and ask them two questions: what does this company do, and what would you do next if you needed them? If they hesitate on either, you've found your problem, and it isn't traffic.

What to do first

  1. Check the phone numberVisible before scrolling, on every page, tappable. This is a fifteen-minute fix and often the highest-value one.
  2. Rewrite the headlineWhat you do, where, and one concrete detail. Plain language beats clever every time.
  3. Put a number on the pageA starting price or a range. Whatever you're comfortable with, but something.
  4. Run PageSpeed InsightsFree, thirty seconds, and it tells you whether speed is even part of your problem.

Only after those four is it worth thinking about more traffic. Getting more people to a page that doesn't convert is the most expensive mistake in small business marketing, and it's the one most commonly sold.

Common questions

Why is my website not getting any calls?

Usually one of six things: the phone number isn't visible without scrolling, the headline doesn't say what you do, there's no price anywhere, all your services share one page, the site is slow on mobile, or there's no single obvious next step. The first four are free to check and take about twenty minutes.

Does more traffic fix a website that isn't converting?

No. If a hundred visitors produce no calls, two hundred will produce no calls twice as fast. Fix the gap between arriving and acting first, then spend on traffic. Buying visitors for a page that doesn't convert is the most common expensive mistake in small business marketing.

Should I put prices on my website?

In most cases yes. Not knowing whether they can afford you is the most common reason someone leaves a small business site, and they generally won't call to find out. A starting figure or typical range removes that hesitation and filters out enquiries you didn't want anyway.
