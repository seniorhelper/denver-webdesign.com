AI search

Your next customer may never see a results page

For twenty years the job was ranking in a list of links. That's still true, and it's no longer the whole picture. A growing share of people ask a question and get an answer, with two or three businesses named in it.

6 min read · Denver Web Design

Somebody needing a contractor now has options that didn't exist a few years ago. They can ask ChatGPT and get three names with reasons. They can read an AI summary at the top of Google and never scroll. They can ask their phone out loud in the car and hear exactly one answer.

In each case a machine read some websites, decided which businesses were worth mentioning, and summarised them. If it couldn't read yours, you weren't in the running.

Why this failure is invisible

A lost ranking shows up in your analytics. Being left out of an AI answer shows up as nothing at all. No impression, no bounce, no data point. The business is simply quieter than it should be, and there's nothing in any report explaining why.

That's what makes it worth checking deliberately, because it won't surface on its own.

The two most common causes

You're blocked, probably by accident

A great many small business sites block AI crawlers without the owner knowing. A security plugin adds the rule. A host enables it by default. A theme ships with a robots file nobody has read since installation.

Check by visiting your domain followed by /robots.txt. If crawlers are listed with Disallow: /, they're being turned away at the door.

There's nothing worth quoting

The crawler gets in, reads the page, and finds atmosphere. "Committed to excellence since 1998." "Passionate about customer service." No prices, no service area, no timelines, no answers to anything anyone asked.

There's nothing to lift, so nothing gets lifted. A competitor who wrote "projects from $4,500, most finished in under two weeks" gets named instead, because that sentence can actually be repeated.

What makes a page quotable

Language models favour pages that state facts plainly, answer a specific question near a heading that asks it, and carry structured data confirming who and where the business is. They struggle with pages that bury the answer three paragraphs into a story.

That's a writing discipline more than a technical one. Short sentences. Real numbers. The question as a heading, the answer immediately underneath.

Conveniently, it's also how people prefer to read. The optimisation and the readability point the same direction, which is unusual enough to be worth taking advantage of.

Check it in two minutes

Open ChatGPT or Perplexity. Ask for a recommendation in your trade and your city the way a customer would. Then ask it directly what it knows about your business by name. Whatever comes back is roughly what your potential customers are getting.

Should you block AI crawlers instead?

Some publishers do, and for them it's defensible — their content is the product, and having it summarised without a visit is a direct loss.

For a local service business the maths runs the other way. You want to be recommended. Blocking the assistants means being left out of answers where a competitor gets named. It's worth thinking about rather than assuming, but for most businesses the conclusion is clear.

What this doesn't mean

It doesn't replace ranking on Google, and be sceptical of anyone selling it that way. Organic results and the local map pack still drive most local business traffic by a wide margin.

It's an additional channel that's growing, and the work overlaps heavily with good search practice anyway — fast pages, clear structure, real answers. Almost nothing you'd do for AI visibility is wasted if you're wrong about how big it gets.

The floor is simply this: being readable costs nothing, and being invisible is a choice you probably didn't make on purpose.

Common questions

How do I get my business mentioned by ChatGPT?

Three things need to be true: AI crawlers must be allowed rather than blocked in your robots file, your pages need structured data stating who you are and where you work, and your content has to state facts plainly enough to be quoted. Most small business sites fail at least one, usually the first, usually by accident.

Should I block AI crawlers from my website?

For publishers whose content is the product, blocking can make sense. For a local service business it usually doesn't, because you want to be recommended. Blocking means being left out of answers where a competitor gets named instead.

Is AI search replacing Google?

No. Organic results and the local map pack still drive the large majority of local business traffic. AI visibility is an additional and growing channel, and the work it requires overlaps almost entirely with good search practice, so very little of it is wasted either way.
