What to put on your homepage: a layout that turns visitors into calls

Your homepage has about one screen to answer three questions. Get those right and the rest of the page can do its job. Here's what goes where, top to bottom.

8 min read · By Zach Wennstedt · Updated October 9, 2026

Short answer: A local business homepage should answer three questions in the first screen: what you do, where you do it, and how to reach you. Below that, list your main services, show proof such as reviews and real photos, explain how the process works, share prices or ranges if you can, answer common questions, and repeat the call to action.

Most homepages are written from the inside out. They start with the company's story, its values, a welcome message. Visitors arrive from the outside in. They have a problem, a location and a deadline, and they want to know quickly whether you're the answer.

Check your first screen

Open your homepage on your phone. Don't scroll. Tick what you can see right now.

Above-the-fold checker

0 of 8

1. The first screen: what, where, how

The headline should say what you do in words a customer would use. "Emergency plumbing in Denver" beats "Excellence in every pipe." Add your area, a tappable phone number and one main button. That's it. Everything else can wait for the scroll.

Sliders, autoplay videos and big mission statements mostly get in the way. If an image doesn't help someone decide, it's costing you load time.

2. Services, one line each

A short list of your main services, each linking to its own page. This helps visitors self-select, and it helps Google understand what you offer. Six clear services with their own pages will outperform one long services page almost every time.

3. Proof

Reviews with names, photos of real jobs, the number of years you've been in business, licenses and memberships. Put proof close to the buttons, because that's where doubt shows up. A strong review next to "Call now" does more than ten reviews on a separate page.

4. How it works

Three or four steps: call or request a quote, we visit or review, you get a price, we do the work. People are more likely to start something when they can see the whole path.

5. Prices or price ranges

If you can share them, do. Ranges filter out poor-fit inquiries and give serious buyers confidence. If every job is unique, explain what moves the price instead. Silence on price is what sends people to the competitor who answered it.

6. Answers to common questions

Pull the questions you hear on the phone every week and answer them on the page. It saves you time, and well-written answers are exactly what search engines and AI assistants quote.

7. The call to action, again

End with the same clear action you started with. Someone who scrolled to the bottom is interested. Don't make them scroll back up to find your number.

The phone test

Hand your phone to someone who doesn't know your business, open your homepage, and ask them what you do and how they'd contact you. If they need more than five seconds, the first screen needs work.

Common homepage mistakes

  • "Welcome to our website" as the headline. It wastes the most valuable line on the page.
  • The phone number only in the footer. Mobile visitors won't go looking for it.
  • A long form above the fold. Ask for a name and phone first; the rest can come later.
  • Everything at once. Ten buttons is the same as none.

Common questions

What should be on a small business homepage?

A headline that says what you do and where, a tappable phone number, one main call to action, a list of services, proof such as reviews and photos, how the process works, prices or ranges if possible, answers to common questions, and a closing call to action.

What does above the fold mean?

It is the part of a page visible before scrolling. On a phone that is a small area, so it should carry the headline, location, phone number and main button.

Should I use a slider on my homepage?

Usually not. Most visitors never see the later slides, and sliders slow pages down. A single strong image and a clear headline work better.

How long should a homepage be?

Long enough to answer the questions customers ask before they call, in a clear order. Length is fine when each section earns its place and the call to action repeats.
